# 3 Coordinate geometry

## Coordinates and drawing lines
- A point is written (x, y): across first, then up.
- To draw y = −2x + 5, make a table of values, plot at least three points and rule a line.
- **Extended**: for 3x + 2y = 5, find two points, such as x = 1 giving y = 1, and x = 3 giving y = −2.

## Gradient
- **Gradient = change in y ÷ change in x.**
- A line going up from left to right has a positive gradient. A line going down has a negative gradient.
- **Extended, from two points**: (1, 2) and (5, 10) give a gradient of (10 − 2) ÷ (5 − 1) = **2**.

## Equation of a line
**y = mx + c**, where m is the gradient and c is the y-intercept.
- y = 6x + 3 has gradient 6 and y-intercept 3.
- x = k is a vertical line. y = k is a horizontal line.
- **Extended**: rearrange to find m. 3x + 2y = 5 becomes y = −1.5x + 2.5, so the gradient is −1.5.

**Finding the equation**: a line has gradient 2 and passes through (1, 5).
- y = 2x + c
- 5 = 2 × 1 + c, so c = 3
- Equation: **y = 2x + 3**

## Parallel lines
- Parallel lines have the **same gradient**.
- Example: the line parallel to y = 4x − 1 through (1, −3).
- y = 4x + c, and −3 = 4 + c, so c = −7.
- Equation: **y = 4x − 7**

## Length and midpoint (Extended)
For (1, 2) and (5, 10):
- **Length** = √((5 − 1)² + (10 − 2)²) = √80 = **8.94**
- **Midpoint** = ((1 + 5)/2, (2 + 10)/2) = **(3, 6)**

## Perpendicular lines (Extended)
- The gradients multiply to −1: m₁ × m₂ = −1.
- A line perpendicular to one with gradient 2 has gradient **−½**.
- Example: the perpendicular bisector of (1, 2) and (5, 10) passes through (3, 6) with gradient −½. So y = −½x + c, and 6 = −1.5 + c, giving **y = −½x + 7.5**.

## Exam tips and common mistakes
- Give equations fully simplified, as y = mx + c unless asked for another form.
- Keep y-difference over x-difference, and use the same order for both.
- Check your line with a point.
