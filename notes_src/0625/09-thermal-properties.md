# 9 Thermal properties and temperature

## Thermal expansion
- At constant pressure, solids, liquids and gases expand when heated.
- Gases expand most and solids expand least.
- **Extended**: in a solid, particles are held tightly by strong forces. In a liquid the forces are weaker. In a gas the particles are far apart with almost no forces between them, so gases expand most.

**Uses and consequences**
- Gaps in bridges and railway tracks allow for expansion on hot days.
- Overhead cables are left slack so they do not snap when they contract in cold weather.
- Liquid-in-glass thermometers work because the liquid expands.
- A metal lid can be loosened by warming it.

## Specific heat capacity
- A rise in an object's temperature increases its **internal energy**.
- **Extended**: this is an increase in the average kinetic energy of all the particles.
- **Specific heat capacity** is the energy required per unit mass per unit temperature increase.
- **c = ΔE / (mΔθ)**, so **ΔE = mcΔθ**. Unit: J/(kg °C).

**Worked example (Extended)**: heat 2.0 kg of water from 20 °C to 70 °C, with c = 4200 J/(kg °C). ΔE = 2.0 × 4200 × 50 = **210 000 J**.

## Measuring specific heat capacity (Extended)
**Solid**: use a metal block with holes for a heater and a thermometer. Wrap it in insulation.

**Liquid**: put a known mass of liquid in an insulated beaker with a heater and a thermometer. Stir it.

**Method for both**
1. Measure the mass and the starting temperature.
2. Switch on the heater for a measured time.
3. Find the energy from E = IVt, or from a joulemeter.
4. Record the highest temperature reached.
5. Calculate c = ΔE / (mΔθ).

The result is usually too high, because some energy escapes to the surroundings.

## Melting and boiling
- During melting and boiling, energy goes in but the **temperature does not change**. The energy separates the particles.
- At standard atmospheric pressure, water melts at **0 °C** and boils at **100 °C**.
- **Condensation**: gas particles cool, slow down and come closer together to form a liquid.
- **Solidification**: liquid particles cool and settle into fixed positions.

## Evaporation
- More-energetic particles escape from the surface of a liquid.
- The particles left behind have a lower average kinetic energy, so the liquid **cools**.

**What speeds up evaporation (Extended)**
- higher temperature
- larger surface area
- more air movement over the surface

**Boiling compared with evaporation (Extended)**

| Boiling | Evaporation |
|---|---|
| happens at the boiling point | happens at any temperature |
| happens throughout the liquid, with bubbles | happens only at the surface |
| needs a constant energy supply | cools the liquid |

**Cooling by evaporation (Extended)**: an object touching an evaporating liquid cools because energy passes from the object to the liquid to replace the energy that was lost. Sweat cools the skin in this way.

## Exam tips and common mistakes
- Temperature stays constant during a change of state, even though energy is going in.
- In ΔE = mcΔθ, Δθ is the temperature change, not the final temperature.
- Evaporation is explained by the fastest particles leaving.

## Quick self-check
1. Why do railway tracks have gaps? *(To allow for expansion in hot weather.)*
2. Find the energy to heat 0.50 kg of aluminium by 10 °C, with c = 900 J/(kg °C). *(4500 J)*
3. Why does evaporation cool a liquid? *(The most energetic particles escape, so the average kinetic energy falls.)*
