AS & A Level Mathematics 9709

4 Mechanics

Take g = 10 m s⁻² unless the question says otherwise. (Cambridge 9709 uses g = 10.)

4.1 Forces and equilibrium

  • Resolve forces into two perpendicular directions. A force F at angle θ to the horizontal has components F cos θ (horizontal) and F sin θ (vertical).
  • Equilibrium: the resultant force is zero, so the sum of the components in each direction is 0.
  • Weight: W = mg, acting vertically downwards.
  • Normal reaction R: perpendicular to the surface.
  • Friction: F ≤ μR. It is limiting (F = μR) when the object is on the point of slipping or is moving. It acts opposite to the motion, or to the direction it would move.
  • On a slope at angle α: the component of weight down the slope = mg sin α, and perpendicular to the slope = mg cos α (so R = mg cos α if nothing else acts perpendicular to the slope).
  • Smooth means there is no friction. Light means the mass is negligible. An inextensible string has the same tension throughout and gives connected particles the same acceleration.

4.2 Kinematics of motion in a straight line

  • Constant acceleration: use SUVAT (v = u + at, s = ut + ½at², v² = u² + 2as, s = ½(u + v)t).
  • Variable acceleration: v = ds/dt, a = dv/dt = d²s/dt², s = ∫v dt, v = ∫a dt (use the initial conditions to find the constants).
  • Velocity–time graph: the gradient = acceleration; the area = displacement (count area below the axis as negative displacement; for total distance, add the sizes).
  • "Comes to instantaneous rest" means v = 0. "Greatest speed" means a = 0 (or check the endpoints).

4.3 Momentum

  • Momentum = mv. Conservation: total momentum before = total momentum after (in a collision or explosion, with no external impulse).
  • If the particles coalesce (stick together), they move with a common velocity afterwards.
  • Take care with directions: choose positive one way and keep to it.

4.4 Newton's laws of motion

  • F = ma, where F is the resultant force in the direction of the acceleration.
  • Connected particles (a string over a pulley, or a car and trailer):
  • write F = ma for each particle separately, then solve simultaneously; or

  • treat the whole system as one body to find a (the tension is internal and cancels), then use one particle to find T.

  • Example (Atwood): masses of 3 kg and 2 kg hang over a smooth pulley. 3g − T = 3a and T − 2g = 2a. Adding: g = 5a, so a = 2 m s⁻² and T = 2(10 + 2) = 24 N.

  • Lift problems: R − mg = ma (accelerating upwards, the apparent weight increases).

4.5 Energy, work and power

  • Work done by a force: W = Fd cos θ (d = distance moved, θ = angle between the force and the motion).
  • KE = ½mv²; GPE = mgh.
  • Work–energy principle: work done by the driving force − work done against resistance = change in KE + change in GPE.
  • Power: P = Fv (driving force × velocity) = work done / time.
  • At maximum (constant) speed, the driving force = the resistance (plus mg sin α on a hill), so the acceleration is 0.
  • Example: a car of mass 1200 kg works at 30 kW against a resistance of 600 N. Find its acceleration when its speed is 20 m s⁻¹. Driving force = 30 000/20 = 1500 N. Resultant force = 1500 − 600 = 900 N. a = 900/1200 = 0.75 m s⁻².

Exam tips and common mistakes

  • Draw a force diagram for each particle, and mark the direction of acceleration.
  • Friction is μR only when the object is limiting or moving.
  • Use the resultant force in F = ma, not just the driving force.
  • In energy questions, list every energy change and the work done against resistance before writing the equation.

Quick self-check

  1. A 5 kg box rests on a rough plane inclined at 30°, on the point of slipping. Find μ. (tan 30° = 0.577)
  2. A particle has v = 3t² − 12t + 9. Find the times when it is at rest. (t = 1 and t = 3)
  3. A 2 kg ball at 6 m s⁻¹ hits a 4 kg ball at rest, and they move off together. Find their common speed. (2 m s⁻¹)
