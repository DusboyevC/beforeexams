AS & A Level Physics 9702

9 Electricity

Key definitions

  • Electric current: the rate of flow of charge: I = ΔQ/Δt.
  • Coulomb: the charge that passes a point when a current of 1 A flows for 1 s.
  • Charge is quantised: it exists only in whole multiples of the elementary charge e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C.
  • Potential difference (p.d.): the energy transferred from electrical to other forms per unit charge: V = W/Q.
  • Volt: 1 joule per coulomb.
  • Electromotive force (e.m.f.): the energy transferred from other forms to electrical energy per unit charge, when charge moves round a complete circuit.
  • Resistance: the ratio of the p.d. across a component to the current through it: R = V/I.
  • Ohm: 1 volt per ampere.
  • Ohm's law: the current in a metallic conductor is proportional to the p.d. across it, provided the temperature is constant.
  • Resistivity (ρ): R = ρL/A. It is a property of the material.

Current in a conductor

  • I = Anvq: A = cross-sectional area, n = number density of charge carriers, v = mean drift velocity, q = charge on each carrier.
  • A thinner section of the same wire carries the same current, so the drift velocity there is higher.

Power

  • P = VI = I²R = V²/R.
  • Energy = Pt = VIt.

I–V characteristics

  • Ohmic metallic conductor (fixed temperature): a straight line through the origin, so R is constant.
  • Filament lamp: a curve that gets less steep at higher V. As the current rises, the filament gets hotter, the lattice ions vibrate more, collisions with electrons are more frequent, so resistance increases.
  • Semiconductor diode: allows current in one direction only (forward bias above about 0.6 V); in reverse bias the resistance is very high.
  • Thermistor (NTC): resistance falls as temperature rises, because more charge carriers are released.
  • Light-dependent resistor (LDR): resistance falls as light intensity rises.

Resistivity experiment

  1. Measure the diameter of the wire with a micrometer at several places; take the mean and find A = πd²/4.
  2. Measure the resistance (V/I, using an ammeter and voltmeter) for several lengths L.
  3. Plot R against L. The gradient = ρ/A, so ρ = gradient × A.
  4. Use a small current, or switch off between readings, to stop the wire heating up.

Worked example

A copper wire 10 m long with diameter 0.50 mm has resistivity 1.7 × 10⁻⁸ Ω m. Find its resistance. A = π(0.25 × 10⁻³)² = 1.96 × 10⁻⁷ m²; R = 1.7 × 10⁻⁸ × 10 / 1.96 × 10⁻⁷ = 0.87 Ω

Exam tips and common mistakes

  • E.m.f. versus p.d.: e.m.f. is energy changed into electrical energy; p.d. is energy changed from electrical energy, both per unit charge.
  • Ohm's law needs "constant temperature" (and other physical conditions).
  • Explain resistance changes in terms of lattice vibrations (filament lamp) or the number of charge carriers (thermistor, LDR).
  • Use the radius (d/2) when calculating the area.

Quick self-check

  1. 4.0 × 10¹⁹ electrons pass a point in 8.0 s. Find the current. (0.80 A)
  2. Explain why the resistance of a filament lamp increases as the current increases.
  3. A 12 V heater has a power of 48 W. Find its resistance. (3.0 Ω)
