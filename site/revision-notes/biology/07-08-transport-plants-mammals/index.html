7 Transport in plants

Structure

  • Xylem: dead, hollow, lignified vessel elements with no end walls, forming continuous tubes. They carry water and mineral ions upwards and give support. Pits allow lateral movement.
  • Phloem: living sieve tube elements (with sieve plates and few organelles) and companion cells (many mitochondria; they load sucrose). They carry assimilates (sucrose, amino acids) in both directions.

Movement of water

  • Pathways across the root: the apoplast pathway (through the cell walls) and the symplast pathway (through the cytoplasm and plasmodesmata).
  • Casparian strip (suberin) in the endodermis blocks the apoplast pathway, so water must enter the symplast. This lets the plant control which minerals enter.
  • Transpiration: the loss of water vapour from the leaves, mainly through the stomata.
  • Cohesion–tension theory: water evaporates from the mesophyll cells and diffuses out of the stomata. This lowers the water potential, and water is pulled up the xylem as a continuous column, because of cohesion (hydrogen bonds between water molecules) and adhesion (to the xylem walls), under tension.
  • Factors that increase transpiration: higher temperature, lower humidity, more air movement (wind), and higher light intensity (stomata open). It is measured with a potometer (which measures water uptake).
  • Xerophytes (adaptations to reduce water loss): a thick waxy cuticle, sunken stomata, rolled leaves, hairs (trap humid air), and fewer stomata.

Translocation

  • Mass flow from source (e.g. leaves) to sink (roots, fruits, growing points): 1. Companion cells actively load sucrose into the sieve tubes (H⁺ ions are pumped out, and sucrose returns with them by co-transport). 2. This lowers the water potential in the sieve tube, so water enters by osmosis from the xylem, and the hydrostatic pressure rises. 3. The sap flows to the sink, where the sucrose is unloaded and used or stored.

8 Transport in mammals

Blood vessels

Vessel Structure Reason
Artery Thick walls with elastic fibres and smooth muscle; narrow lumen Withstands high pressure; recoils to smooth the flow
Vein Thin walls; wide lumen; valves Low pressure; valves prevent backflow
Capillary One cell thick (endothelium); narrow Short diffusion distance for exchange
  • Tissue fluid: formed at the arterial end of capillaries, where the hydrostatic pressure is greater than the oncotic pressure, so fluid and small molecules are forced out. At the venous end it returns by osmosis. The excess drains into the lymph.

Blood

  • Red blood cells: biconcave (a large surface area : volume ratio); no nucleus (more room for haemoglobin).
  • Haemoglobin and the oxygen dissociation curve: the curve is S-shaped (sigmoid), because of cooperative binding: once one O₂ binds, the shape changes and the next O₂ binds more easily.
  • Bohr shift: at higher CO₂ (lower pH), haemoglobin has a lower affinity for O₂, so the curve shifts right and more O₂ is unloaded in respiring tissues.
  • CO₂ transport: carbonic anhydrase in red blood cells catalyses CO₂ + H₂O → H₂CO₃ → H⁺ + HCO₃⁻. HCO₃⁻ diffuses out into the plasma and Cl⁻ moves in (the chloride shift). H⁺ binds to haemoglobin (forming haemoglobinic acid), which releases O₂. Some CO₂ forms carbaminohaemoglobin.
  • Fetal haemoglobin has a higher affinity for O₂, so it takes O₂ from the mother's blood.

The heart

  • Cardiac cycle: atrial systole → ventricular systole (the AV valves close; the semilunar valves open) → diastole.
  • Control: the SAN (pacemaker) sends a wave of excitation across the atria, which contract. The AVN delays it, then passes it down the Purkyne tissue in the septum (the bundle of His) to the apex, so the ventricles contract from the base upwards.
  • The left ventricle wall is thicker, because it pumps blood at higher pressure around the whole body.

Exam tips and common mistakes

  • In cohesion–tension answers, mention evaporation, cohesion, tension, and the continuous column.
  • Translocation: active loading → lower water potential → water enters → hydrostatic pressure → mass flow.
  • Explain the Bohr shift in terms of affinity and unloading in respiring tissues.

Quick self-check

  1. Explain the role of the Casparian strip.
  2. Describe how tissue fluid is formed.
  3. Explain why the AVN delays the impulse.
