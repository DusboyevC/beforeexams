9 Gas exchange

Structure of the gas exchange system

Tissue Trachea Bronchi Bronchioles Alveoli
Cartilage (keeps the airway open) C-shaped rings Plates None None
Smooth muscle Yes Yes Yes (controls the diameter) None
Ciliated epithelium and goblet cells Yes Yes Some None
Elastic fibres Yes Yes Yes Yes (recoil during expiration)
- Goblet cells secrete mucus, which traps dust and pathogens; cilia beat to move the mucus up to the throat.
- Alveoli are adapted for gas exchange: a large surface area; thin walls (squamous epithelium, one cell thick), so the diffusion distance is short; a good blood supply and ventilation keep the concentration gradient steep; and surfactant prevents collapse.

Measles Virus Airborne droplets
  • Antibiotics: kill or inhibit bacteria, e.g. penicillin weakens the cell wall by inhibiting peptidoglycan cross-linking, so the bacterial cell bursts. They do not work against viruses.
  • Antibiotic resistance: a random mutation gives resistance. When the antibiotic is used, resistant bacteria survive and reproduce (natural selection) and pass the resistance allele on (including by plasmid transfer). Reduce it by not overusing antibiotics and by completing the course.

11 Immunity

Cells

  • Phagocytes (neutrophils, macrophages): engulf pathogens (phagocytosis) and digest them with lysosomal enzymes. Macrophages act as antigen-presenting cells.
  • B-lymphocytes: mature in the bone marrow. When activated, they divide into plasma cells, which secrete antibodies, and memory cells.
  • T-lymphocytes: mature in the thymus. T-helper cells release cytokines that stimulate B cells and phagocytes. T-killer cells destroy infected cells.
  • Primary response: slow (clonal selection and expansion take time). Secondary response: faster and larger, because memory cells are already present.

Antibodies

  • Y-shaped proteins: 4 polypeptide chains (2 heavy, 2 light) held by disulfide bonds, with variable regions forming antigen-binding sites that are complementary to a specific antigen, and a hinge region for flexibility.
  • They work by agglutination (clumping pathogens together), neutralising toxins, and marking pathogens for phagocytosis (opsonisation).
  • Monoclonal antibodies: identical antibodies made by one clone of hybridoma cells. Used in diagnosis (e.g. pregnancy tests) and treatment (targeting cancer cells).

Types of immunity

Natural Artificial
Active (your own antibodies and memory cells; long-term) After an infection Vaccination
Passive (antibodies given; short-term, no memory cells) Across the placenta or in breast milk Injection of antibodies
- Vaccination: introduces antigens (from a dead or weakened pathogen, or a toxoid), which stimulates a primary response and the formation of memory cells. Herd immunity: when enough of the population is vaccinated, spread of the disease is limited.
- Autoimmune disease (e.g. myasthenia gravis): the immune system attacks the body's own cells.

Exam tips and common mistakes

  • Gas exchange: link each feature to diffusion (surface area, distance, gradient).
  • Resistance arises by mutation, not because "bacteria get used to" the antibiotic.
  • Passive immunity provides no memory cells, so it is short-term.

Quick self-check

  1. Explain the role of goblet cells and cilia.
  2. Describe how penicillin kills bacteria.
  3. Explain why the secondary response is faster than the primary response.
