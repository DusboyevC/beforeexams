2 Biological molecules

Carbohydrates

  • Monosaccharides: glucose (α and β isomers, differing in the position of the –OH on carbon 1), fructose, galactose, ribose.
  • Glycosidic bond: formed by a condensation reaction (water is removed); broken by hydrolysis.
  • Disaccharides: maltose (glucose + glucose), sucrose (glucose + fructose; non-reducing), lactose (glucose + galactose).
Polysaccharide Structure Function / property
Starch: amylose α-glucose, 1,4 bonds, a helix Compact energy store in plants
Starch: amylopectin α-glucose, 1,4 and 1,6 branches Many ends for rapid hydrolysis
Glycogen α-glucose, 1,4 and more 1,6 branches Animal energy store; very compact; fast to hydrolyse
Cellulose β-glucose, 1,4 bonds; alternate units rotated 180° Straight chains linked by hydrogen bonds into microfibrils; high tensile strength (plant cell walls)
  • Starch and glycogen are good stores because they are insoluble (no osmotic effect), compact, and easily hydrolysed to glucose.

Lipids

  • Triglyceride: glycerol + 3 fatty acids joined by ester bonds (condensation reactions).
  • Saturated fatty acids have no C=C; unsaturated fatty acids have C=C bonds that kink the chain.

  • Functions: energy store (more energy per gram than carbohydrate, because of many C–H bonds), insulation, buoyancy, protection, and a source of metabolic water.

  • Phospholipid: glycerol + 2 fatty acids + a phosphate group. It has a hydrophilic head and hydrophobic tails, so it forms bilayers in membranes.

Proteins

  • Amino acid: an amine group, a carboxyl group, and an R group on the central carbon. Peptide bonds form by condensation.
  • Primary structure: the sequence of amino acids.
  • Secondary: an α-helix or β-pleated sheet, held by hydrogen bonds between the C=O and N–H of the backbone.
  • Tertiary: further folding into a specific 3D shape, held by hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, disulfide bonds (between cysteines), and hydrophobic interactions between R groups.
  • Quaternary: two or more polypeptide chains (e.g. haemoglobin), sometimes with a prosthetic group (e.g. haem).
  • Globular proteins (e.g. haemoglobin, enzymes): roughly spherical; soluble (hydrophilic R groups on the outside); metabolic roles.

  • Haemoglobin: 4 polypeptides (2α, 2β), each with a haem group containing Fe²⁺ that binds O₂.

  • Fibrous proteins (e.g. collagen): long, insoluble, structural.

  • Collagen is a triple helix of three polypeptides (every third amino acid is glycine, the smallest, allowing tight coiling). The molecules are cross-linked into fibrils and fibres, giving high tensile strength (in skin, tendons and bone).

Water

  • Water is polar: it forms hydrogen bonds between molecules.
  • Properties linked to its importance:
  • a solvent for polar and ionic substances (transport and reactions);
  • a high specific heat capacity (a stable temperature for organisms and habitats);
  • a high latent heat of vaporisation (cooling by sweating or transpiration);
  • cohesion (transpiration stream; surface tension);
  • ice is less dense than water (it floats and insulates the water below).

Exam tips and common mistakes

  • Glycosidic, ester and peptide bonds all form by condensation and break by hydrolysis.
  • Link the structure of cellulose (β-glucose, H-bonds, microfibrils) to its strength.
  • For the non-reducing sugar test, include every step, including neutralisation.

Quick self-check

  1. Describe how the structure of glycogen suits it to its function.
  2. Name the bonds that stabilise the tertiary structure of a protein.
  3. Explain why triglycerides are a better energy store than glycogen.
