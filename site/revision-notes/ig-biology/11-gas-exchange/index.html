11 Gas exchange in humans

Gas exchange surfaces

Good gas exchange surfaces have:

  • a large surface area, from many alveoli
  • a thin surface, for a short diffusion distance
  • a good blood supply, from many capillaries
  • good ventilation with air

The breathing system

Air passes through the larynx, trachea, bronchi, bronchioles and into the alveoli. Associated parts: lungs, diaphragm, ribs, intercostal muscles and capillaries.

Extended: cartilage in the trachea keeps the airway open.

Breathing in and out (Extended)

Internal intercostal muscles relax contract, in forced breathing

Inspired and expired air

Gas Inspired air Expired air
Oxygen about 21% about 16%
Carbon dioxide about 0.04% about 4%
Water vapour variable saturated
  • Test: breathe out through limewater. It turns milky faster than with inspired air.

Why they differ (Extended)

  • Less oxygen: it is absorbed into the blood for respiration.
  • More carbon dioxide: it is made by respiration and excreted.
  • More water vapour: it evaporates from the moist surfaces of the alveoli.

Exercise and breathing

  • Physical activity increases the rate and depth of breathing.
  • Extended: respiring muscles produce more carbon dioxide. The increased concentration in the blood is detected by the brain, leading to faster and deeper breathing.

Protecting the system (Extended)

  • Goblet cells make mucus, which traps pathogens and particles.
  • Ciliated cells have cilia that move the mucus up to the throat, where it is swallowed.

Exam tips and common mistakes

  • Air moves because of a pressure difference. The lungs do not suck air in.
  • The external intercostals contract when you breathe in.
  • Trachea, bronchi and bronchioles come in that order.

Quick self-check

  1. Name two features of alveoli that help gas exchange. (Large surface area and thin walls)
  2. What happens to the diaphragm when you breathe in? (It contracts and flattens.)
  3. Why does exercise increase breathing rate? (Blood CO₂ rises, and the brain detects this.)
