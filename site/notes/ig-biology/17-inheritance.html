17 Inheritance

Chromosomes, genes and alleles

  • Chromosomes are made of DNA, which contains genetic information in the form of genes.
  • A gene is a length of DNA that codes for a protein.
  • An allele is an alternative form of a gene.
  • Sex in humans: females are XX and males are XY. Each child has a 50% chance of being either.

How proteins are made (Extended)

  • The sequence of bases in a gene determines the sequence of amino acids used to make a protein.
  • Different sequences of amino acids give proteins different shapes.
  • DNA controls cell function by controlling the production of proteins, including enzymes, membrane carriers and neurotransmitter receptors.

Making a protein

  1. The gene stays in the nucleus.
  2. Messenger RNA (mRNA), a copy of the gene, is made in the nucleus.
  3. The mRNA moves to the cytoplasm and passes through ribosomes.
  4. The ribosome assembles amino acids into a protein.
  5. The order of amino acids is set by the sequence of bases in the mRNA.

Most body cells contain the same genes, but many are not expressed, because a cell only makes the proteins it needs.

Haploid and diploid (Extended)

  • A haploid nucleus has a single set of chromosomes.
  • A diploid nucleus has two sets.
  • A human diploid cell has 23 pairs of chromosomes.

Mitosis

  • Mitosis is nuclear division giving genetically identical cells.
  • Its roles are growth, repair of damaged tissues, replacement of cells and asexual reproduction.

Extended

  • Chromosomes are exactly replicated before mitosis.
  • During mitosis the copies separate, keeping the chromosome number the same in each daughter cell.
  • Stem cells are unspecialised cells that divide by mitosis, giving daughter cells that can become specialised.

Meiosis (Extended)

  • Meiosis is involved in making gametes.
  • It is a reduction division: the chromosome number is halved from diploid to haploid, giving genetically different cells.

Key terms

  • Inheritance: transmission of genetic information from generation to generation.
  • Genotype: the genetic make-up of an organism, in terms of the alleles present.
  • Phenotype: the observable features.
  • Homozygous: two identical alleles of a gene. Two identical homozygous individuals breed true (they are pure-breeding).
  • Heterozygous: two different alleles. A heterozygous individual is not pure-breeding.
  • Dominant allele: expressed if it is present.
  • Recessive allele: expressed only when no dominant allele is present.

Monohybrid crosses

Example: T = tall, t = short. Cross Tt × Tt.

T t
T TT Tt
t Tt tt

Phenotype ratio: 3 tall : 1 short.

Cross Tt × tt gives a 1 : 1 ratio.

Pedigree diagrams: if two unaffected parents have an affected child, the condition is recessive and both parents are heterozygous.

Test cross (Extended)

To find an unknown dominant genotype, cross it with a homozygous recessive.

  • All offspring show the dominant feature: the unknown is homozygous.
  • About half show the recessive feature: the unknown is heterozygous.

Codominance (Extended)

  • Codominance: both alleles in a heterozygote contribute to the phenotype.
  • ABO blood groups: alleles Iᴬ, Iᴮ and Iᵒ. Iᴬ and Iᴮ are codominant, and Iᵒ is recessive.

Sex linkage (Extended)

  • A sex-linked characteristic has its gene on a sex chromosome, so it is more common in one sex.
  • Red-green colour blindness is recessive, on the X chromosome. Males have only one X, so one recessive allele causes it.

Cross a carrier mother XᴺXⁿ with a normal father XᴺY:

Xᴺ Y
Xᴺ XᴺXᴺ XᴺY
Xⁿ XᴺXⁿ XⁿY

Offspring: normal girl, carrier girl, normal boy, colour-blind boy.

Exam tips and common mistakes

  • Use a capital and a small form of the same letter.
  • Show the parents, gametes, offspring genotypes and phenotypes.
  • A 3 : 1 ratio is a probability, so real results vary.

Quick self-check

  1. How many chromosomes in a human gamete? (23)
  2. Which division gives genetically identical cells? (Mitosis)
  3. Why is colour blindness more common in males? (They have only one X chromosome.)
