9 Transport in animals

Circulatory systems

  • A circulatory system is a system of blood vessels with a pump and valves to ensure one-way flow of blood.
  • Fish: single circulation. Blood passes through the heart once per circuit: heart → gills → body → heart.
  • Mammals: double circulation. Blood passes through the heart twice: heart → lungs → heart → body → heart.

Advantages of a double circulation (Extended)

  • Blood is pumped at high pressure to the body after picking up oxygen, so oxygen reaches cells faster.
  • Lower pressure to the lungs prevents damage to the thin capillaries.
  • Oxygenated and deoxygenated blood do not mix.

The heart

Parts: muscular wall, septum, left and right atria, left and right ventricles, one-way valves, coronary arteries.

Blood flow: vena cava → right atrium → right ventricle → pulmonary artery → lungs → pulmonary vein → left atrium → left ventricle → aorta → body.

  • Blood is pumped away from the heart in arteries and returns in veins.
  • Heart activity can be monitored by ECG, pulse rate and listening to the valves closing.

Extended

  • The left ventricle has a thicker wall than the right, because it pumps blood to the whole body. The right only pumps to the nearby lungs.
  • Ventricles have thicker walls than atria, because the atria only push blood into the ventricles.
  • The septum keeps oxygenated and deoxygenated blood apart.
  • Atrioventricular valves are between atria and ventricles. Semilunar valves are in the aorta and pulmonary artery.

One heartbeat (Extended)

  1. Atria contract, pushing blood into the ventricles.
  2. Ventricles contract. Atrioventricular valves close, so blood cannot go back.
  3. Blood leaves through the semilunar valves.
  4. Ventricles relax. Semilunar valves close.

Exercise and heart rate

  • Physical activity increases the heart rate.
  • Extended: muscles respire faster, so they need more oxygen and glucose, and must lose more carbon dioxide. The heart beats faster to supply this.

Coronary heart disease

  • The coronary arteries supply the heart muscle. CHD is their blockage.
  • Risk factors: diet, lack of exercise, stress, smoking, genetic predisposition, age and sex.
  • Reducing risk: a diet low in saturated fat and regular exercise.

Blood vessels

Vessel Wall Lumen Other
Artery thick, muscular, elastic narrow carries blood at high pressure
Vein thin wide has valves, low pressure
Capillary one cell thick very narrow exchanges substances with tissues

Extended

  • Arteries have thick walls to withstand high pressure.
  • Veins have a wide lumen and valves, because the pressure is low.
  • Capillaries have thin walls for fast diffusion and form a large network with a large surface area.

Main vessels

  • Heart: vena cava, aorta, pulmonary artery, pulmonary vein.
  • Lungs: pulmonary artery and pulmonary vein.
  • Kidney: renal artery and renal vein.
  • Extended, liver: hepatic artery, hepatic veins and hepatic portal vein.

Blood

Component Function
Red blood cells transport oxygen, using haemoglobin
White blood cells phagocytosis and antibody production
Platelets clotting
Plasma transports blood cells, ions, nutrients, urea, hormones and carbon dioxide
  • Clotting prevents blood loss and the entry of pathogens.

Extended

  • Lymphocytes produce antibodies.
  • Phagocytes engulf pathogens by phagocytosis.
  • Clotting: fibrinogen is converted to fibrin, which forms a mesh.

Exam tips and common mistakes

  • The left of the heart appears on the right side of a diagram.
  • Arteries carry blood away from the heart, not always oxygenated. The pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood.
  • Name the chambers and vessels in the correct order.

Quick self-check

  1. Which vessel has valves? (Vein)
  2. Why is the left ventricle wall thicker? (It pumps blood at higher pressure around the whole body.)
  3. What does haemoglobin carry? (Oxygen)
