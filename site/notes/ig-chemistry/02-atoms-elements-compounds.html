2 Atoms, elements and compounds

Elements, compounds and mixtures

  • Element: made of one type of atom.
  • Compound: two or more elements chemically combined in a fixed ratio.
  • Mixture: two or more substances not chemically combined, so they can be separated physically.

Atomic structure

The atom has a central nucleus of protons and neutrons, surrounded by electrons in shells.

  • Proton (atomic) number: the number of protons in the nucleus.
  • Mass (nucleon) number: the total number of protons and neutrons.
  • Electronic configuration: shells hold 2, 8, 8. Example: aluminium (13) is 2,8,3.
  • Outer shell electrons = group number, for Groups I to VII.
  • Number of occupied shells = period number.
  • Group VIII noble gases have a full outer shell.

Isotopes

  • Isotopes are different atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons.
  • Extended: isotopes have the same chemical properties, because they have the same number of electrons and so the same electronic configuration.

Relative atomic mass from isotopes (Extended) Chlorine is 75% ³⁵Cl and 25% ³⁷Cl. Aᵣ = (35 × 75 + 37 × 25) ÷ 100 = 35.5

Ionic bonding

  • Cations are positive ions, made when atoms lose electrons. Metals form cations.
  • Anions are negative ions, made when atoms gain electrons. Non-metals form anions.
  • An ionic bond is a strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions.
  • Example: Na (2,8,1) gives one electron to Cl (2,8,7), forming Na⁺ (2,8) and Cl⁻ (2,8,8). Show this with a dot-and-cross diagram.
  • Extended: ionic compounds form a giant lattice, a regular arrangement of alternating positive and negative ions.

Properties of ionic compounds

  • High melting and boiling points: strong attractions between many ions need a lot of energy to overcome.
  • Good conductors when molten or aqueous: the ions can move.
  • Poor conductors when solid: the ions are fixed in place.

Covalent bonding

  • A covalent bond forms when a pair of electrons is shared between two atoms, leading to noble gas configurations.
  • Core dot-and-cross examples: H₂, Cl₂, H₂O, CH₄, NH₃, HCl.
  • Extended examples: CH₃OH, C₂H₄, O₂ and CO₂ (double bonds), N₂ (triple bond).

Simple molecular compounds

  • Low melting and boiling points: the forces between molecules are weak (Extended: weak intermolecular forces). The covalent bonds do not break.
  • Poor conductors: there are no ions or free electrons to carry charge.

Silicon(IV) oxide, SiO₂ (Extended): each Si bonds to 4 O atoms, and each O bonds to 2 Si atoms. Like diamond, it is hard, has a very high melting point and does not conduct, because of its giant structure of strong covalent bonds.

Metallic bonding (Extended)

  • Metallic bonding is the electrostatic attraction between positive ions in a giant lattice and a 'sea' of delocalised electrons.
  • Good electrical conductors: delocalised electrons can move.
  • Malleable and ductile: layers of ions can slide over each other without breaking the bonding.

Exam tips and common mistakes

  • Do not say ionic compounds have molecules.
  • For simple molecules, melting breaks the forces between molecules, not the bonds inside them.
  • Graphite conducts because of delocalised electrons between layers.

Quick self-check

  2. Give the configuration of Ca²⁺. (2,8,8)
  3. Why does solid NaCl not conduct? (Its ions are held in fixed positions.)
