7 Acids, bases and salts

Reactions of acids

  • Acid + metal → salt + hydrogen
  • Acid + base → salt + water
  • Acid + carbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide

Examples:

  • Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂
  • CuO + H₂SO₄ → CuSO₄ + H₂O
  • CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂

Bases and alkalis

  • Bases are oxides or hydroxides of metals.
  • Alkalis are soluble bases.
  • Base + acid → salt + water
  • Base + ammonium salt → salt + water + ammonia

Indicators

Indicator Acid Alkali
Litmus red blue
Thymolphthalein colourless blue
Methyl orange red yellow

pH and ions

  • Aqueous acids contain H⁺ ions. Aqueous alkalis contain OH⁻ ions.
  • Universal indicator paper shows the pH by its colour. pH below 7 is acidic, pH 7 is neutral and pH above 7 is alkaline.
  • A lower pH means a higher H⁺ concentration.
  • Neutralisation: H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l)

Strong and weak acids (Extended)

  • Acids are proton donors. Bases are proton acceptors.
  • A strong acid is completely dissociated in aqueous solution: HCl(aq) → H⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq)
  • A weak acid is partially dissociated: CH₃COOH(aq) ⇌ H⁺(aq) + CH₃COO⁻(aq)

Oxides

  • Acidic oxides are oxides of non-metals, such as SO₂ and CO₂.
  • Basic oxides are oxides of metals, such as CuO and CaO.
  • Amphoteric oxides (Extended) react with acids and with bases to give a salt and water. Examples: Al₂O₃ and ZnO.

Solubility rules

  • Sodium, potassium and ammonium salts are soluble.
  • Nitrates are soluble.
  • Chlorides are soluble, except lead and silver.
  • Sulfates are soluble, except barium, calcium and lead.
  • Carbonates are insoluble, except sodium, potassium and ammonium.
  • Hydroxides are insoluble, except sodium, potassium, ammonium and calcium (partially).

Making soluble salts

Excess metal, insoluble base or insoluble carbonate

  1. Warm the acid.
  2. Add the solid until no more reacts. This shows it is in excess.
  3. Filter to remove the excess solid.
  4. Heat the filtrate to evaporate some water, until crystals start to form.
  5. Leave it to crystallise, then filter off the crystals and dry them between filter papers.

Example: copper(II) oxide + sulfuric acid gives copper(II) sulfate.

Acid and alkali by titration

  1. Use a pipette to put alkali in a flask. Add an indicator.
  2. Add acid from a burette until the colour just changes. Note the volume.
  3. Repeat with the same volumes and no indicator.
  4. Evaporate and crystallise.

Use this method for sodium, potassium and ammonium salts.

Making insoluble salts by precipitation (Extended)

  1. Mix two soluble salts, each with one ion of the wanted salt.
  2. Filter off the precipitate.
  3. Wash with distilled water and dry.

Example: barium chloride + sodium sulfate gives barium sulfate. Ionic equation: Ba²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → BaSO₄(s)

Hydrated and anhydrous substances

  • A hydrated substance is chemically combined with water.
  • An anhydrous substance contains no water.
  • Extended: water of crystallisation is the water molecules in hydrated crystals, as in CuSO₄•5H₂O and CoCl₂•6H₂O.

Exam tips and common mistakes

  • Strong and concentrated do not mean the same thing. Strong refers to how much the acid dissociates.
  • Add excess solid so that no acid remains.
  • Do not evaporate to dryness if you want hydrated crystals.

Quick self-check

  1. Name the salt made from zinc and hydrochloric acid. (Zinc chloride)
  2. Name the gas given off by an ammonium salt and an alkali. (Ammonia)
  3. Why is an excess of solid added when making a salt? (To make sure all the acid has reacted.)
