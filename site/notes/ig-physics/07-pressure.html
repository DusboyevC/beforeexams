7 Pressure

What pressure is

  • Pressure is force per unit area: p = F / A.
  • Units: Pa, where 1 Pa = 1 N/m². N/cm² is also used.

Everyday examples

Large area gives low pressure

  • Snowshoes and skis stop you sinking into snow.
  • Wide tractor tyres stop the tractor sinking into mud.
  • Wide bag straps feel more comfortable.

Small area gives high pressure

  • A sharp knife cuts easily.
  • A drawing pin goes into wood with a small push.
  • Stiletto heels can mark soft floors.

Pressure in liquids

  • Pressure beneath the surface of a liquid increases with depth.
  • Pressure also increases with the density of the liquid.
  • This is why a dam is built thicker at the bottom.
  • Extended: the change in pressure is Δp = ρgΔh.

Worked examples

Solid: a 600 N box has a base area of 0.30 m². p = 600 / 0.30 = 2000 Pa.

Liquid (Extended): find the extra pressure 12 m below the surface of water. Density = 1000 kg/m³ and g = 9.8 N/kg. Δp = 1000 × 9.8 × 12 = 118 000 Pa, or about 120 kPa.

Changing the area: the same box stands on a smaller face of area 0.10 m². The force is unchanged, so the pressure is three times bigger: 6000 Pa.

Exam tips and common mistakes

  • Convert area carefully: 1 m² = 10 000 cm², not 100 cm².
  • Use weight, in N, as the force. If you are given mass, multiply by g first.
  • In ρgΔh, use depth in metres and density in kg/m³.
  • Pressure in a liquid depends on depth, not on the shape of the container.

Quick self-check

  1. A force of 50 N acts on 2.0 m². Find the pressure. (25 Pa)
  2. Why does a camel have wide feet? (The wide area lowers the pressure, so it sinks less into sand.)
  3. Find the pressure change 3.0 m down in oil of density 800 kg/m³. (About 23 500 Pa)
