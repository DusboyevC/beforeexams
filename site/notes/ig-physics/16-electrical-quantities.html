16 Electrical quantities

Electric charge

  • There are positive and negative charges. Like charges repel and unlike charges attract.
  • Charging by friction: rubbing a polythene rod with a cloth moves electrons from one to the other. Only negative charge moves. The object that gains electrons becomes negative, and the one that loses them becomes positive.
  • Showing charge: a charged rod attracts small pieces of paper, or moves a thin stream of water.
  • Extended: charge is measured in coulombs (C).

Conductors and insulators

  • Conductors, such as metals, have free electrons that can move. Insulators, such as plastic, rubber and glass, have electrons that are held in place.
  • Test: connect a cell, a lamp and two wires with a gap. Place the material in the gap. If the lamp lights, it is a conductor.

Electric fields (Extended)

  • An electric field is a region in which an electric charge experiences a force.
  • Its direction at a point is the direction of the force on a positive charge.
  • Point charge or charged conducting sphere: radial lines, outward for positive and inward for negative.
  • Two oppositely charged parallel plates: evenly spaced parallel lines from positive to negative.

Electric current

  • Electric current is related to the flow of charge. In metals it is the movement of free electrons.
  • Extended: current is the charge passing a point per unit time: I = Q / t. Unit: A.
  • Extended: conventional current flows from positive to negative. Electrons flow from negative to positive.
  • d.c. flows in one direction only. a.c. keeps reversing direction.
  • An ammeter measures current. It is connected in series. Choose a suitable range.

e.m.f. and p.d.

  • e.m.f. is the electrical work done by a source in moving a unit charge around a complete circuit. Unit: V.
  • p.d. is the work done by a unit charge passing through a component. Unit: V.
  • Extended: E = W / Q and V = W / Q.
  • A voltmeter measures p.d. It is connected in parallel.

Resistance

  • R = V / I. Unit: Ω.
  • Experiment: connect the component with an ammeter in series and a voltmeter in parallel. Record V and I, and divide.
  • A longer wire has more resistance. A thicker wire has less resistance.
  • Extended: resistance is directly proportional to length and inversely proportional to cross-sectional area.

Current–voltage graphs (Extended)

  • Constant resistor: straight line through the origin.
  • Filament lamp: a curve that gets flatter. As the lamp gets hotter, its resistance increases.
  • Diode: current flows in one direction only. Very high resistance the other way.

Electrical energy and power

  • Circuits transfer energy from a source, such as a cell or mains supply, to components and then to the surroundings.
  • P = IV
  • E = IVt
  • The kilowatt-hour (kW h) is the energy used by a 1 kW appliance in 1 hour.

Worked example: a 2.0 kW heater runs for 3.0 hours at 30 cents per kW h. Energy = 6.0 kW h. Cost = 6.0 × 30 = 180 cents.

Exam tips and common mistakes

  • Ammeters go in series and voltmeters go in parallel.
  • Only electrons move when charging by friction. Positive charge does not move.
  • Convert minutes to seconds in E = IVt, but use hours for kW h.

Quick self-check

  1. A charge of 30 C passes in 60 s. Find the current. (0.50 A)
  2. A lamp has 12 V across it and 2.0 A through it. Find R and P. (6.0 Ω, 24 W)
  3. Why does a filament lamp's graph curve? (It gets hotter, so its resistance increases.)
