17 Electric circuits and electrical safety

Components

You need to draw and recognise cells, batteries, power supplies, generators, potential dividers, switches, fixed and variable resistors, heaters, thermistors, LDRs, lamps, motors, bells, ammeters, voltmeters, magnetising coils, transformers, fuses and relays. Extended: also diodes and LEDs.

  • Thermistor (NTC): resistance falls as temperature rises.
  • LDR: resistance falls as light gets brighter.
  • Relay: a small current switches a larger current on or off.
  • Diode: lets current through in one direction only. An LED also gives out light (Extended).

Series circuits

  • The current is the same at every point.
  • Combined e.m.f. of sources in series = the sum of the e.m.f.s.
  • Combined resistance = R₁ + R₂ + …
  • Extended: the total p.d. is the sum of the p.d.s across the components.

Parallel circuits

  • The current from the source is larger than the current in each branch.
  • The combined resistance of two resistors in parallel is less than either one alone.
  • Extended: the sum of currents into a junction equals the sum of currents out, because charge is conserved.
  • Extended: the p.d. across each branch is the same.
  • Extended: 1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂.

Why lamps in a lighting circuit are in parallel

  • Each lamp can be switched on and off separately.
  • If one lamp fails, the others stay on.
  • Each lamp gets the full supply voltage.

Worked example (Extended): 6.0 Ω and 3.0 Ω in parallel. 1/R = 1/6 + 1/3 = 1/2, so R = 2.0 Ω.

Potential dividers

  • For a constant current, a larger resistance has a larger p.d. across it.
  • Extended: for two resistors in series, R₁ / R₂ = V₁ / V₂.
  • Extended: a variable potential divider uses a variable resistor, thermistor or LDR. Changing its resistance changes how the supply p.d. is shared.

Worked example (Extended): a 12 V supply with R₁ = 2.0 kΩ and R₂ = 4.0 kΩ. The ratio is 1 : 2, so V₁ = 4.0 V and V₂ = 8.0 V.

Electrical safety

Hazards

  • damaged insulation: risk of electric shock
  • overheating cables: risk of fire
  • damp conditions: risk of shock, as water conducts
  • overloaded plugs, extension leads and sockets: excess current causes overheating and fire

Mains wiring

  • Three wires: live (line), neutral and earth.
  • The switch must be in the live wire, so that the appliance is disconnected from the high voltage when it is off.

Fuses and trip switches

  • A fuse melts and breaks the circuit if the current is too high.
  • A trip switch (circuit breaker) opens automatically and can be reset.
  • Choose a fuse rating just above the normal current. Example: a 2300 W kettle on 230 V takes 10 A, so use a 13 A fuse rather than 3 A.

Casing of appliances

  • A metal case must be earthed. If a live wire touches the case, a large current flows to earth and the fuse blows, so the case is not left live.
  • A double-insulated appliance has a non-conducting case, so it needs no earth wire. Its fuse still protects the circuit and the cabling.

Exam tips and common mistakes

  • Current does not get used up. It is the same before and after a lamp.
  • Combined parallel resistance is always smaller than the smallest resistor.
  • A fuse protects the wiring from overheating. It does not directly protect the person.

Quick self-check

  1. Find the total resistance of 4 Ω, 6 Ω and 10 Ω in series. (20 Ω)
  2. Find the combined resistance of two 8 Ω resistors in parallel. (4 Ω)
  3. Why is the switch in the live wire? (So the appliance is not connected to the live voltage when it is off.)
