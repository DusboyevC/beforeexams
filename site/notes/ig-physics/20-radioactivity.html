20 Radioactivity

Background radiation

Background radiation is always around us. Sources include:

  • radon gas in the air
  • rocks and buildings
  • food and drink
  • cosmic rays

Measuring radiation

  • Ionising radiation is measured with a detector connected to a counter.
  • Count rate is in counts/s or counts/minute.
  • Extended: corrected count rate = measured count rate − background count rate.

The three emissions

Emission from a nucleus is spontaneous and random in direction.

Alpha (α) Beta (β) Gamma (γ)
Nature helium nucleus (2 protons, 2 neutrons) high-speed electron electromagnetic radiation
Charge +2 −1 0
Ionising effect strong medium weak
Penetration stopped by paper stopped by thin aluminium reduced by thick lead

Why they differ (Extended): alpha has a large charge and high kinetic energy, so it ionises strongly and loses energy quickly. Gamma has no charge, so it ionises weakly and penetrates far.

In electric and magnetic fields (Extended)

  • Alpha and beta are deflected in opposite directions, because their charges are opposite.
  • Beta is deflected more, because its mass is much smaller.
  • Gamma is not deflected.

Radioactive decay

  • Radioactive decay is a change in an unstable nucleus. It is spontaneous and random.
  • In alpha or beta decay, the nucleus changes to that of a different element.
  • Extended: isotopes may be radioactive because of too many neutrons, or because the nucleus is too heavy.
  • Extended: decay makes the nucleus more stable. In beta decay, neutron → proton + electron, which reduces the number of excess neutrons.

Decay equations (Extended)

  • Alpha: A falls by 4 and Z falls by 2. ²²⁶₈₈Ra → ²²²₈₆Rn + ⁴₂α
  • Beta: A stays the same and Z rises by 1. ¹⁴₆C → ¹⁴₇N + ⁰₋₁β
  • Gamma: A and Z do not change. The nucleus only loses energy.

Half-life

  • Half-life is the time taken for half the nuclei of that isotope in any sample to decay.
  • Worked example: a sample has a count rate of 800 counts/s and a half-life of 2 hours. After 6 hours (3 half-lives): 800 → 400 → 200 → 100 counts/s.
  • Extended: if background has not been subtracted, subtract it first. Then read the half-life from the decay curve.

Choosing an isotope (Extended)

Use Radiation Half-life and reason
Smoke alarm alpha long; alpha ionises air and is stopped by smoke
Irradiating food gamma long enough; kills bacteria and passes through packaging
Sterilising equipment gamma long; passes through packaging
Thickness control beta for paper, gamma for steel long; only partly absorbed by the sheet
Cancer diagnosis and treatment gamma short, so the dose is limited; it gets out of the body

Safety

  • Ionising radiation can cause cell death, mutations and cancer.
  • Move radioactive sources with long tongs, and store them in lead-lined boxes.
  • Extended: reduce exposure time, increase distance and use shielding.

Exam tips and common mistakes

  • Subtract background before finding half-life.
  • Radiation emission is random, so count rates change slightly.
  • Gamma has no mass number or charge change.

Quick self-check

  1. Which radiation is stopped by paper? (Alpha)
  2. A sample falls from 1600 to 200 counts/s in 9 days. Find the half-life. (3 days)
  3. What happens to Z in beta decay? (It increases by 1.)
