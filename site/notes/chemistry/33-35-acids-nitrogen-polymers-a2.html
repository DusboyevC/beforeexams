33 Carboxylic acids and derivatives (A Level)

  • Acidity: chloroethanoic acids are stronger than ethanoic acid. The electronegative Cl atoms withdraw electrons and stabilise the carboxylate ion; more Cl atoms make the acid stronger. Alkyl groups (electron-donating) make acids weaker.
  • Acyl chlorides (RCOCl): made from RCOOH + PCl₅, PCl₃ or SOCl₂. They are very reactive (the carbonyl C is strongly δ+).
    • water → carboxylic acid + HCl (white fumes; the reaction is vigorous at room temperature).
    • alcohol → ester + HCl (faster, and not reversible, compared with using the acid).
    • phenol → phenyl ester (phenol does not react directly with carboxylic acids).
    • ammonia → amide; + amine → N-substituted amide.

  • Mechanism: addition–elimination. The nucleophile attacks the C=O, then Cl⁻ is lost.

  • Ease of hydrolysis: acyl chloride > alkyl chloride > aryl chloride.

34 Nitrogen compounds (A Level)

  • Basicity: ethylamine > ammonia > phenylamine.
  • In ethylamine, the alkyl group donates electrons, increasing the electron density on N, so its lone pair is more available to accept H⁺.

  • In phenylamine, the lone pair on N is delocalised into the benzene ring, so it is less available.

  • Phenylamine: made by reducing nitrobenzene with Sn + concentrated HCl (heat), then adding NaOH.

    • bromine water → a white precipitate of 2,4,6-tribromophenylamine.

  • Diazotisation and azo dyes: phenylamine + HNO₂ (NaNO₂ + HCl) below 10 °C gives a benzenediazonium ion. This couples with phenol in alkaline solution to form an azo dye (–N=N–), which is coloured because of the extended delocalised system. Above 10 °C, the diazonium ion decomposes, releasing N₂.

  • Amides: neutral (the lone pair on N is delocalised over the C=O). They are hydrolysed by acid or alkali to the acid (or its salt) and the amine.
  • Amino acids: H₂N–CHR–COOH. In solution they exist as zwitterions (H₃N⁺–CHR–COO⁻), which gives high melting points and solubility in water. The isoelectric point is the pH at which the amino acid has no net charge.
  • Two amino acids join through a peptide (amide) bond in a condensation reaction.
  • Electrophoresis: amino acids or peptides move in an electric field according to their charge at the buffer pH (and their size).

35 Polymerisation (A Level)

  • Condensation polymerisation: monomers with two functional groups join and a small molecule (H₂O or HCl) is lost.
  • Polyesters: a diol + a dicarboxylic acid, e.g. PET (Terylene).

  • Polyamides: a diamine + a dicarboxylic acid (or diacyl dichloride), e.g. nylon-6,6. Proteins are natural polyamides (polypeptides).

  • Biodegradability: polyesters and polyamides can be hydrolysed (by acid, alkali or enzymes) at the ester or amide links, so they are biodegradable, unlike poly(alkenes).

  • Identifying the monomer: split the polymer at the ester or amide link and add back H₂O (–OH to the C=O, and H to the O or N).

Exam tips and common mistakes

  • Explain basicity using the availability of the lone pair on N.
  • In the azo coupling question, give the temperature (below 10 °C) and the conditions (alkaline phenol).
  • In condensation polymers, draw the repeat unit with one ester or amide link and the extension bonds.

Quick self-check

  1. Explain why phenylamine is a weaker base than ammonia.
  2. Draw the repeat unit of the polyamide made from hexane-1,6-diamine and hexanedioic acid.
  3. Describe what happens when ethanoyl chloride is added to water.
