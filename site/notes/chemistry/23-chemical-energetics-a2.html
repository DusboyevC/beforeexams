23 Chemical energetics (A Level)

Key definitions

  • Lattice energy (ΔH_latt): the enthalpy change when one mole of an ionic compound is formed from its gaseous ions under standard conditions (exothermic): Na⁺(g) + Cl⁻(g) → NaCl(s).
  • Enthalpy change of atomisation (ΔH_at): the enthalpy change when one mole of gaseous atoms is formed from the element in its standard state.
  • First electron affinity (EA₁): the enthalpy change when one electron is added to each atom in one mole of gaseous atoms to form one mole of gaseous 1− ions (usually exothermic). EA₂ is endothermic, because a negative ion repels the incoming electron.
  • Enthalpy change of hydration (ΔH_hyd): the enthalpy change when one mole of gaseous ions dissolves in water to give an infinitely dilute solution (exothermic).
  • Enthalpy change of solution (ΔH_sol): the enthalpy change when one mole of an ionic solid dissolves in water to give an infinitely dilute solution.
  • Entropy (S): a measure of the disorder of a system, i.e. the number of possible arrangements of the particles and their energy. S(gas) > S(liquid) > S(solid).

Born–Haber cycle (e.g. NaCl)

ΔH_f = ΔH_at(Na) + IE₁(Na) + ΔH_at(Cl) + EA₁(Cl) + ΔH_latt

  • Use it to calculate the lattice energy (or any one missing term).
  • The lattice energy is more exothermic when the ions are smaller and have a higher charge, because the electrostatic attraction is stronger. For example, MgO is far more exothermic than NaCl.

Enthalpy change of solution

  • ΔH_sol = −ΔH_latt + ΣΔH_hyd.
  • ΔH_hyd is more exothermic for smaller, more highly charged ions (stronger attraction to the polar water molecules).
  • Group 2 sulfates become less soluble down the group: the lattice energy and the hydration enthalpy both become less exothermic, but the hydration enthalpy decreases more (the large sulfate ion dominates the lattice energy), so ΔH_sol becomes more endothermic.

Entropy and Gibbs free energy

  • ΔS° = ΣS°(products) − ΣS°(reactants) (units J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹).
  • Entropy increases when: a solid melts or a liquid boils; a solid dissolves; the number of gas molecules increases; the temperature rises.
  • ΔG° = ΔH° − TΔS°. Convert ΔS to kJ (÷ 1000) to match ΔH.
  • A reaction is feasible (spontaneous) when ΔG ≤ 0.
  • If ΔH > 0 and ΔS > 0, the reaction becomes feasible above T = ΔH/ΔS.
  • A feasible reaction can still be slow, because of a high activation energy (a kinetic barrier).

Worked example

For CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂: ΔH = +178 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔS = +161 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. Find the minimum temperature for decomposition. T = 178 / 0.161 = 1106 K

Exam tips and common mistakes

  • Each definition needs one mole, the correct states (gaseous ions for lattice energy), and standard conditions.
  • Born–Haber: watch the stoichiometry, e.g. 2 × the Cl atomisation for MgCl₂, and IE₁ + IE₂ for Mg.
  • Units: ΔS is in J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ but ΔH is in kJ mol⁻¹.

Quick self-check

  1. Explain why the lattice energy of MgO is more exothermic than that of NaF.
  2. Predict the sign of ΔS for N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) → 2NH₃(g). (negative)
  3. Explain why a reaction with ΔH < 0 and ΔS < 0 stops being feasible at high temperature.
