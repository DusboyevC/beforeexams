5 The mitotic cell cycle

Chromosomes

  • A chromosome is DNA wound around histone proteins. After replication it has two identical sister chromatids joined at the centromere.
  • Telomeres: repeated non-coding sequences at the ends of chromosomes that prevent the loss of genes each time DNA is replicated.
  • Stem cells: undifferentiated cells that can divide by mitosis and differentiate into specialised cells (cell replacement and tissue repair).

The cell cycle

  • Interphase: G1 (growth; protein and organelle synthesis), S (DNA replication), G2 (growth; preparation for division).
  • Mitosis (nuclear division), then cytokinesis (division of the cytoplasm).
  • Importance of mitosis: produces genetically identical cells for growth, repair, replacing cells, and asexual reproduction.

Stages of mitosis

  1. Prophase: chromosomes condense and become visible as two chromatids; the nuclear envelope breaks down; centrioles move to the poles and the spindle forms.
  2. Metaphase: chromosomes line up at the equator, attached to spindle fibres at their centromeres.
  3. Anaphase: the centromeres divide; the spindle fibres shorten and pull the sister chromatids to opposite poles.
  4. Telophase: chromosomes reach the poles and uncoil; nuclear envelopes re-form. - Uncontrolled mitosis caused by mutations in genes that control the cell cycle can lead to a tumour (cancer).

6 Nucleic acids and protein synthesis

Structure

  • Nucleotide: a pentose sugar + a phosphate + a nitrogenous base. Nucleotides join by phosphodiester bonds in condensation reactions.
  • DNA: a double helix of two antiparallel strands held together by hydrogen bonds between complementary base pairs: A–T (2 hydrogen bonds) and C–G (3). Its sugar is deoxyribose.
  • RNA: single-stranded, with ribose, and uracil instead of thymine. Types: mRNA, tRNA, rRNA.
  • Purines (A, G) have two rings; pyrimidines (C, T, U) have one.

Semi-conservative replication

  1. DNA helicase breaks the hydrogen bonds and the double helix unwinds.
  2. Each strand acts as a template.
  3. Free nucleotides pair with their complementary bases.
  4. DNA polymerase joins the nucleotides (forming phosphodiester bonds) in the 5′→3′ direction. One strand is made continuously (leading strand) and the other in fragments (lagging strand), which are joined by DNA ligase.
  5. Each new molecule has one original strand and one new strand.

Protein synthesis

  • Gene: a sequence of nucleotides that codes for a polypeptide. The genetic code is a triplet code: three bases (a codon) code for one amino acid. It is degenerate (more than one codon per amino acid), non-overlapping and universal.
  • Transcription (in the nucleus): RNA polymerase uses one DNA strand as a template to make mRNA. In eukaryotes, the pre-mRNA is spliced: introns are removed and the exons are joined.
  • Translation (at a ribosome): the mRNA codon pairs with the complementary anticodon on a tRNA carrying a specific amino acid. Peptide bonds form between adjacent amino acids, and the ribosome moves along the mRNA until it reaches a stop codon.
  • Gene mutation: a change in the base sequence.
  • Substitution may change one amino acid, or none (because the code is degenerate).
  • Insertion or deletion causes a frameshift, changing every codon after it, so it is usually more harmful.
  • Example: sickle cell anaemia is caused by a base substitution in the β-globin gene (glutamic acid is replaced by valine).

Exam tips and common mistakes

  • In anaphase, the centromeres split and the chromatids are pulled apart, so they move with the centromere first (a V shape).
  • In replication, name the enzymes and say why it is semi-conservative.
  • Distinguish codon (on mRNA) from anticodon (on tRNA).

Quick self-check

  1. Describe what happens during the S phase.
  2. Explain why a deletion usually affects the polypeptide more than a substitution does.
  3. A DNA strand reads TAC GGA. Give the mRNA it is transcribed into. (AUG CCU)
